The journal

Some stones hold more than light.

A jewel can carry a record of discovery, mistaken identity, taste and power. These three famous gems are a starting point for looking at the stories inside the sparkle.

Stories from gem history · 4 minute read

In this guide

  1. The Hope Diamond
  2. The Black Prince’s Ruby
  3. The Logan Sapphire
Ask Rich
01

Diamond · From India to the Smithsonian

The Hope Diamond

A deep-blue diamond with a journey from India to the French royal collection and, eventually, the Smithsonian. A larger predecessor became the French Blue; after the theft of the French Crown Jewels in 1792, a smaller blue diamond appeared in London in 1812. It would take the Hope name from collector Henry Philip Hope.

Harry Winston, Inc. donated the gem to the Smithsonian in 1958. Its arrival was remarkably ordinary for such an extraordinary object: it travelled by registered mail in a plain package.

Look beyond the celebrity and notice its deep body colour. This is a very different way to appreciate diamond from searching for the most colourless stone. The Smithsonian also documents its red phosphorescence, another reminder that a gem’s response to light can be part of its scientific character.

Worth asking: A diamond’s appeal is not limited to colourlessness. Fancy-colour diamonds have their own language of beauty.

Further reading: Smithsonian · The Hope Diamond

02

Red spinel · Britain’s Imperial State Crown

The Black Prince’s Ruby

The large red jewel in the front cross of Britain’s Imperial State Crown is a spinel. Its familiar name survives from a time when similar-looking red gems were not always distinguished by their mineral identity.

Historic Royal Palaces relates the tradition that Pedro of Castile gave it to Edward, the Black Prince, in 1367 after a military victory. Stories also place it in Henry V’s helmet at Agincourt. These early associations are presented as tradition and legend, rather than an unbroken modern ownership record.

The jewel’s identity makes its story more interesting. An exceptional red stone can earn its place in history on its own merits, even when the name attached to it turns out to be wrong.

Worth asking: Colour alone cannot identify a gemstone. Spinel deserves to be chosen for what it is.

Further reading: Historic Royal Palaces · The Crown Jewels

03

Sapphire · 423 carats · Sri Lanka

The Logan Sapphire

At 423 carats, the Logan Sapphire is an extraordinary example of Sri Lankan sapphire. Its mixed cushion cut shows blue with a slight violet influence, framed by twenty diamonds in a silver and gold brooch.

Rebecca Pollard Guggenheim gave it to the Smithsonian in 1960, although she retained possession until 1971. By then she had married John A. Logan, giving the gem the name by which it is known today.

The museum records a June 1997 GIA report finding natural colour with no evidence of heat treatment. Here, size, colour, a documented treatment finding and a traceable modern history all add to the interest. Its scale is exceptional; the habit of looking closely at both stone and paperwork applies to a much smaller gem too.

Worth asking: An origin story is richer when it comes with records. Ask what is documented and what remains tradition.

Further reading: Smithsonian · The Logan Sapphire
