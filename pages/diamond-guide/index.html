A beautiful diamond. A considered choice.

The most expensive diamond on paper is not automatically the one you will love most. Understand what you are paying for, then decide which differences matter to you.

Ask Rich
01

Start with what you will notice.

Before comparing reports, decide on a shape, an approximate size on the hand and a comfortable budget for the finished piece. Include the setting in that budget. A stone chosen in isolation can leave too little room for the design you imagined.

The four Cs describe cut, colour, clarity and carat weight. Their rarest combinations command premiums. Some differences change what you see; others are most apparent under controlled grading conditions. Decide whether you are buying for everyday beauty, a particular rarity, or a little of both.

This guide focuses on colourless to light-yellow diamonds. Fancy-colour diamonds, such as natural pinks and blues, need a different conversation about colour and rarity.

Worth asking: Ask for two or three stones at different price points, with an explanation of the difference you can actually see.

Further reading: GIA · Diamond quality factors

02

Cut: spend on the way it handles light.

Shape is the outline: round, oval, pear and so on. Cut quality is about how the facets work together. Brightness is returned white light; fire is the flashes of colour; scintillation is the changing pattern of sparkle as you or the stone moves.

A high colour or clarity grade cannot rescue a lifeless appearance. Start with attractive light performance and compare stones while moving them, rather than judging a single photograph.

GIA’s familiar Excellent-to-Poor overall cut scale applies to standard round brilliant diamonds in the D–Z colour range. Do not assume the same overall grade exists on a report for every fancy shape. Polish and symmetry are useful details, but do not replace looking at the stone.

Worth asking: Protect brightness and an appealing pattern of light and dark before paying for higher grades elsewhere.

Further reading: GIA · Understanding diamond cut

03

Colour: find your own threshold.

On the D–Z scale, D is the colourless end. Moving towards Z, body colour becomes more evident. Rarity can make a higher colour grade more expensive, even when the difference between two stones is subtle to you.

A useful comparison is a colourless stone alongside a near-colourless option, such as G–J, in your chosen shape and size. This is a starting point for looking, not a promise that every near-colourless diamond will look the same once set.

Setting metal affects appearance because a diamond reflects its surroundings. Compare against the actual metal you want. Yellow gold does not automatically hide all warmth, and white metal does not make every diamond look colourless.

Worth asking: If you cannot see a difference that matters to you, ask what the lower colour grade saves.

Further reading: GIA · How setting metals affect appearance

04

Clarity: pay for what your eyes can appreciate.

Clarity describes internal inclusions and surface blemishes. The grading scale runs from Flawless to Included, with assessment at 10× magnification. A flawless report describes rarity; it does not mean every lower-clarity stone will look visibly marked.

“Eye-clean” is a useful conversation, rather than a formal GIA grade. Agree what it means: visible from which angle, at what distance and in which lighting? VS2 or SI1 can be comparison candidates, but neither grade guarantees an eye-clean stone. Shape, size and the position and nature of inclusions all matter.

Ask whether an inclusion affects transparency or creates a durability concern, as well as whether you can see it. Saving on an invisible distinction is different from accepting a distracting mark or structural weakness.

Worth asking: Compare a higher-clarity stone with a lower grade that looks clean to you in normal viewing.

Further reading: GIA · Diamond clarity

05

Carat: buy the presence, not just the number.

Carat is weight, not a measurement of the visible surface. Two diamonds with the same weight can have different face-up dimensions. A deep stone can carry weight you will not see from above.

Prices can rise at familiar weight thresholds, such as one carat. It is worth comparing a stone just below your target with one just above it. The price difference and visible size difference depend on the actual pair; there is no universal saving.

Use the millimetre measurements alongside carat weight and view the stone on the hand. Do not sacrifice good proportions solely to hit a round number or maximise spread.

Worth asking: Ask to compare just below your chosen weight threshold, keeping appearance and cut quality in view.

Further reading: GIA · Understanding carat weight

06

An often misunderstood detail

Fluorescence: a detail worth understanding.

The term is fluorescence: some diamonds emit visible light while exposed to ultraviolet radiation, often producing a blue glow. UV is different from visible blue light, and sunlight contains UV too. A blacklight makes the effect easy to demonstrate, but does not tell you how the diamond will look in everyday settings.

Fluorescence may have little visible effect in ordinary wear. Under UV-containing light it can make some diamonds appear less yellow. GIA research also found reduced light–dark contrast in some strongly fluorescent stones; obvious haze was mainly associated with light-scattering features, which can combine with fluorescence.

Some fluorescent natural diamonds are offered at lower prices than otherwise comparable stones. There is no automatic discount or guarantee of value. Compare the actual quotes and inspect the actual stones in daylight and indoor lighting for brightness, contrast and transparency.

Worth asking: Keep a fluorescent stone on the shortlist if you love its appearance. Neither reject nor choose it on the report description alone.

Further reading: GIA · Research on blue fluorescence

07

Natural origin, clearly documented.

Rich sources natural diamonds. Their formation in the Earth is part of their appeal, and natural origin should be clearly stated in the paperwork for the stone you choose.

Laboratory-grown diamonds have essentially the same chemical, physical and optical properties, but a different growth history. Appearance alone is not a reliable way to distinguish them. Ask how the laboratory report is matched to your natural diamond, and keep the documentation with your purchase. Natural origin does not come with a promise of future resale value.

Worth asking: Ask how the report confirms natural origin and how it is matched to this diamond.

Further reading: GIA · Natural and laboratory-grown diamonds

08

Read the report. Then look at the diamond.

An independent report gives you a basis for comparison. Check the issuing laboratory, report number, measurements, grades and comments, and ask how the document is matched to the stone. For a GIA report, use GIA’s own Report Check service to confirm the recorded details.

A report is not a valuation or a guarantee that you will prefer this stone. Two diamonds with similar grades can still look different. View them under the same conditions and ask for unfiltered video if you cannot see them in person.

Worth asking: Ask Rich to walk through the report with you, including any comments you do not understand.

Further reading: GIA · Report Check

09

Bring these questions to your shortlist.

  • Which difference am I paying for, and can I see it?
  • Could a lower colour or clarity grade still give me the appearance I want?
  • What happens to the price if we look just below my target carat weight?
  • How does this stone look in daylight, ordinary indoor light and the intended setting?
  • Are there inclusions or treatments that affect appearance, care or durability?
  • What is the total for the stone and setting, and what are the agreed terms before I commit?

You do not need to become a gemologist before starting. Bring the things you love and the questions you have; the details can become part of the conversation.

Worth asking: Spend on visible beauty and sound condition. Be flexible about specifications when the difference does not matter to you.
